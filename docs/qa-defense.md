# Pramaan — Finale Q&A Defense

### 1. Your entire thesis is 'score the artifact, not the text.' But the artifact is GitHub. India's hiring funnel is dominated by non-coders, tier-2/3 grads who learned on college LMS, freshers with zero public commits, and product/ops/sales/design roles where the proof isn't a repo at all. For that majority, Pramaan returns 'unverified' on everything. How is a system that goes blind on most Indian candidates a trust layer and not a coder-only toy?
*severity: could sink us*

Honest answer: GitHub is our highest-signal source today, but provenance is a pattern, not a synonym for git. The RESOLVE step is artifact-type-agnostic by design: for a designer it resolves Behance/Dribbble/Figma community files, for a writer it's published bylines via Jina Reader, for an ops/PM person it's a live deployed product, a Notion/Substack with authored timestamps, a Devfolio/Devpost project, a Kaggle profile, an npm/PyPI package. The honest limit is that today's PoC implements the GitHub + live-URL + published-writing fetchers well and the others are stubs. The critical design decision that keeps us defensible: 'unverified' is NOT a penalty, it's a routing signal. A candidate with no resolvable artifacts isn't scored down to zero; they're routed to the human/assessment track the Resume Ranker already uses. Pramaan's job is to confidently lift the verifiable candidates and confidently flag the contradicted ones, not to adjudicate the silent middle. We must be explicit in the demo that coverage expands fetcher-by-fetcher, and that 'no evidence' never equals 'rejected.'

### 2. AI-generated-code detection is statistically unreliable — every academic and commercial detector has high false-positive rates, and post-2023 a huge share of legitimate human work is Copilot-assisted. You say you 'FLAG, never auto-reject.' Then what is the flag actually worth? If a human still has to adjudicate every flag, you've added noise, not signal.
*severity: could sink us*

You're right that AI-code detection as a binary classifier is a loser's game, and we deliberately don't bet on it. We made two design choices because of exactly this. First, the flag is never a verdict input — it cannot move a verdict from verified to unverified; it's metadata attached to a verified card. Second, and more importantly, we don't lean on stylometric 'this looks AI-written' detection at all, because that's the unreliable part. We lean on provenance signals that are hard to fake regardless of who/what wrote the code: commit cadence (50 files in one commit with no incremental history), authorship continuity (does this person have a commit history that builds to this, or did a finished repo appear fully-formed), and timeline reconciliation against the claim. 'AI-assisted' is increasingly the norm and we treat it as neutral — the flag exists to give a human reviewer context, not to penalize. The honest concession: if a sophisticated candidate commits AI-generated code incrementally with realistic cadence, we will not detect it as AI, and we shouldn't — at that point they've done the work of understanding and integrating it, which is the actual skill we care about.

### 3. Walk me through the honeypot kill-shot mechanically, then tell me how I beat it. You say timeline reconciliation kills the ~80 impossible profiles. If I'm a fraudster, I just backdate my git commits — git lets you set author dates to anything. Your whole 'expensive to fake' moat collapses to one --date flag. Defend it.
*severity: could sink us*

This is the most important question and the honeypot detection does NOT rely on author-date, precisely because author-date is attacker-controlled. The kill-shot is cross-source temporal corroboration. Author date in git is trivially forged; what is expensive to forge is the convergence of independent, server-stamped timelines that the candidate doesn't control: GitHub's committer timestamp and the push event in the Events API (server-side, not the spoofable author date), the repo creation date, first-release timestamps on npm/PyPI, the Wayback Machine's first-capture date of a 'live since 2019' deployment, SSL cert issuance dates, and publication dates on indexed writing. The honeypot profiles fail because they assert a claim that requires multiple independent clocks to agree, and the cheap forgery only moves one of them. Example: 'React, 4 years, since 2021' but the GitHub account was created in 2026, the repo's first server-side push is 2026, and the domain's first Wayback capture is 2026. The fraudster can backdate the author date; they cannot retroactively make GitHub's servers, npm, and archive.org agree. Honest limit: a patient adversary who actually built real artifacts over real time is indistinguishable from a real candidate — which is correct, because at that point they did the work. The moat is the asymmetry: faking ONE timeline is cheap, faking the CONVERGENCE of independent server-stamped timelines over years is not.

### 4. You claim the LLM 'reasons over retrieved evidence, never the profile text' and therefore won't hallucinate. That's a hope, not a guarantee. LLMs fabricate citations, misread diffs, and confidently invent commit attributions. If your RECONCILE step hallucinates that a commit proves React when it's actually a config change, you've laundered a false claim into a 'verified' card with a confidence score. What concretely stops that?
*severity: could sink us*

Acknowledged — 'reasons over evidence' reduces but does not eliminate hallucination, and a verified card is more dangerous than no card because it carries authority. Our controls: (1) Every cited_artifact in the Evidence Card must be a real, resolvable URL/SHA that we fetched in the FETCH step — the LLM is constrained to cite only from the retrieved evidence set, and we validate every citation post-hoc by re-checking the SHA/URL exists. A card whose citations don't dereference is rejected before emit. (2) The verdict is structured, not freeform: the model must map a claim to specific evidence items, and we can mechanically check that the cited diff actually touches files/extensions consistent with the skill (a 'React' verdict citing only a README change is auto-downgraded). (3) Confidence is calibrated to be conservative — thin or ambiguous evidence emits 'unverified,' not low-confidence 'verified,' because a false verified is far costlier than a false unverified in hiring. The honest residual risk: the LLM can still misjudge whether a real diff genuinely demonstrates the skill (semantic, not citation, error). Our mitigation is that the plain_language_reason and cited artifacts are human-auditable in the card itself — a recruiter can click through to the exact commit. We are a transparent assist with receipts, not a black-box autodecider, and we'd rather under-claim than launder a false positive.

### 5. Privacy and consent. You're fetching, storing, and running an LLM over a candidate's GitHub history, blog, and deployed sites — building a permanent provenance dossier on them, possibly without an explicit consent step, and under India's DPDP Act 2023 that's personal data processing. What's your legal and ethical basis, and what happens when a candidate says 'delete my graph'?
*severity: could sink us*

Real exposure, and we treat it as a product requirement, not an afterthought. Basis: (1) We only fetch artifacts the candidate themselves listed on their Redrob profile or that are public and self-published — the candidate is pointing us at the evidence, which establishes purpose-limited consent at application time (the Resume Ranker already processes their submitted profile; Pramaan verifies the links they gave). We do not crawl to discover hidden accounts they didn't disclose. (2) Under DPDP 2023 the candidate is the Data Principal; consent must be specific and revocable, so the Redrob application flow needs an explicit 'verify my linked artifacts' opt-in, and the Evidence Cards must be deletable on request — graphs are keyed to the application and purged on withdrawal/erasure request. (3) We store evidence pointers and verdicts, and should minimize raw diff retention (cache with TTL, not permanent dossier). The honest gap in the current PoC: there's no consent UI yet and retention is undefined — that's a Redrob-integration workstream, not solved code today. We'd be lying if we said the PoC is DPDP-compliant; the architecture supports compliance (self-disclosed sources, application-scoped, deletable), but the consent gate and retention policy are roadmap items we'd build with Redrob's legal team before any real candidate data touches it.

### 6. Cost and latency at scale. Redrob processes huge applicant volumes. Per candidate you're doing N skill-resolutions, GitHub API calls, Playwright renders, Jina fetches, Exa searches, AND multiple LLM reconciliation calls. Playwright alone is heavy and slow. GitHub rate-limits you. What's the per-candidate cost and p95 latency, and does this survive a 10,000-applicant funnel without bankrupting Redrob?
*severity: could sink us*

Concrete numbers and an honest admission this is a real engineering constraint. Per-candidate cost drivers: LLM reconciliation is the dollar cost (cheap on OpenRouter — small models like the Llama/Qwen/Gemini-flash tier handle structured evidence reasoning for a fraction of a cent per claim; we don't need a frontier model to check if a diff matches a claim), and GitHub/Jina/Exa are the rate-limit constraint. Our design for scale: (1) Pramaan is NOT run on every applicant synchronously — it runs as an async trust-enrichment job triggered for candidates the Resume Ranker shortlists, so we verify hundreds, not the full top-of-funnel. That single decision changes the cost math by an order of magnitude. (2) Heavy fetchers are tiered: cheap liveness/HEAD checks and GitHub API (authenticated = 5000 req/hr) first; Playwright is the expensive fallback only used when a URL needs JS rendering, not by default. (3) Evidence is cacheable and candidate-stable — a verified commit doesn't change, so re-applications and re-runs hit cache. (4) Verdicts are emitted per-skill so we parallelize and can early-exit. Honest p95: a code-heavy candidate with several claimed skills is seconds-to-low-minutes of async wall-time, dominated by I/O, not a sub-second API. That's fine for a batch trust layer; it would NOT be fine as a synchronous gate, and we don't position it as one. The cost we'd flag to Redrob upfront: bounded LLM spend per shortlisted candidate, and GitHub API quota is the thing to monitor at volume.

### 7. Sell me on why this isn't just a better Resume Ranker feature. You say 'trust layer, not a ranking,' but the moment you emit confidence scores and verified/contradicted verdicts, downstream that IS a ranking signal. Why should Redrob build a separate provenance engine instead of just feeding GitHub data into the ranker they already have?
*severity: could sink us*

Because the unit of work is fundamentally different, and conflating them is how you get the keyword-stuffer problem in the first place. The Resume Ranker is a relevance/matching system: given text, how well does it match the JD. It's optimized to score self-description, which is exactly why it's gameable — anyone can write better self-description. Pramaan is an adjudication system: given a claim, does external evidence support it, with a citation. These have different failure modes, different data sources, different latency profiles (sync ranking vs async verification), and critically different trust semantics — a ranker that absorbs raw GitHub data would just learn 'more repos = higher score,' which is the same gameable text-popularity trap one layer down. By keeping Pramaan a separate layer that emits structured, cited verdicts the ranker CONSUMES (not raw features), you get auditability the ranker can't provide: when a hiring manager asks 'why is this candidate trusted,' there's a clickable commit, not a model weight. The architectural reason for separation is the same reason you separate authentication from authorization: verification of identity-of-work is a distinct concern from ranking-of-fit, and bolting it into the ranker makes both worse. Fair pushback on your point: yes, verdicts become a ranking input downstream — but as a discrete, explainable, human-auditable signal, not as opaque features melted into a score.

### 8. Integration reality. Redrob has a Resume Ranker in production today. How does Pramaan actually plug in without us rewriting their pipeline? What's the contract between your Evidence Cards and their ranker, and what do they do with a 'contradicted' verdict — auto-reject? Because if you're not careful you've just built a defamation machine that wrongly flags real candidates as frauds.
*severity: nice to nail*

The integration is deliberately thin: Pramaan is a service that takes (candidate_id, claimed_skills[], artifact_links[]) and returns Evidence Cards as a structured payload the ranker treats as additional features/annotations. No pipeline rewrite — it's an enrichment call on the shortlist stage. The contract is the Evidence Card schema {skill, verdict, confidence, cited_artifacts[], plain_language_reason}, versioned and stable. On the defamation risk — this is the right worry and our answer is a hard policy: 'contradicted' NEVER auto-rejects. It's a flag that routes to human review with the receipts attached, so a person sees 'claimed React since 2021, but all timelines say 2026' and the actual evidence, then decides. We refuse to be an autonomous reject button for exactly the reason you state — a wrong 'contradicted' on a real candidate is the worst outcome in the system, worse than missing a fraud. That's also why our confidence is asymmetric: we require strong multi-source convergence to emit 'contradicted,' and when evidence is merely thin we emit 'unverified,' not 'contradicted.' The distinction between 'we found no proof' and 'we found disproof' is load-bearing and we never blur it.

### 9. Forked-vs-authored attribution is harder than you're implying. A candidate can fork a popular repo, make trivial commits, and look like a contributor. Or contribute real PRs to an org repo where commits show org attribution. Or rewrite history. GitHub's authorship data is itself manipulable. How robust is your ATTRIBUTE step really, versus how robust does the slide make it look?
*severity: could sink us*

The slide oversimplifies it and I'll be straight about the hard cases. What we do reliably: distinguish fork from original (the API exposes fork status and parent), and check whether the candidate's commits are substantive diffs vs trivial (whitespace/README/version-bump) using the diff content, not just commit count. Where it's genuinely hard: (1) Org/team repos where real contribution is split across many authors — here commit-author attribution actually works in our favor because the committer email/identity is server-recorded, and merged PRs carry the author; we credit authored commits and merged PRs, not repo ownership. (2) The adversarial fork-and-trivially-commit attack — we counter it by weighting on diff substance and authorship continuity, so 100 trivial commits on a fork score far below 5 substantive authored commits, but a determined faker who writes real-looking diffs on a fork is a residual gap. (3) Rewritten history — committer timestamps and the push/Events API are harder to rewrite than author dates, same defense as the honeypot answer. Honest summary: ATTRIBUTE is strong at fork-vs-original and trivial-vs-substantive, weaker against a skilled adversary willing to author genuine diffs on borrowed scaffolding — but that adversary is, again, doing real work, which collapses back into the moat's central asymmetry.

### 10. India/Bharat relevance — the judges care about this. Your evidence sources (GitHub, Jina, Exa, deployed URLs) skew toward English-speaking, internet-fluent, urban candidates with public footprints. How does Pramaan serve a Bharat candidate from a tier-3 town whose best proof is a college project on a shared drive or a WhatsApp-distributed app? Or is this just an elite-coder filter dressed up as fairness?
*severity: nice to nail*

The fairness framing cuts both ways and I want to be precise. The current source mix does skew toward candidates with public footprints — that's a real bias and we should name it rather than dress it up. But the design intent is the opposite of an elite filter: provenance is the MOST democratizing signal available, because it doesn't care which college you attended or how polished your English résumé is — it cares whether you built something. A tier-3 candidate with one genuinely-authored deployed app and real commits will out-verify a metro candidate with a keyword-stuffed résumé and forked repos. That's a leveling, not a filter. The honest gap: candidates whose proof lives in non-indexable places (shared drives, WhatsApp APKs, offline college work) currently resolve to 'unverified,' and 'unverified' must route to assessment, never to rejection — so they're no worse off than under the current ranker, and Pramaan simply abstains. The Bharat-positive roadmap move is expanding fetchers to where Bharat builders actually publish: Play Store listings, Devfolio, regional hackathon archives, and accepting candidate-uploaded artifacts with provenance metadata. The principle we'd defend to judges: Pramaan rewards evidence of work over polish of self-presentation, and that bias favors the substance-over-pedigree candidate — but only if 'no public evidence' is treated as 'verify another way,' never 'fail.'

### 11. Adversarial arms race. The moment Pramaan ships and candidates know GitHub provenance is being scored, a market for 'aged GitHub accounts with real-looking commit history' appears overnight — it already exists for social media. You can buy a 4-year-old account with organic-looking activity. Your asymmetry moat assumes faking is expensive; what happens when someone industrializes the fake?
*severity: could sink us*

This is the strongest long-term threat and I won't pretend the moat is permanent — it's an asymmetry, not an impossibility. Two honest layers of defense. First, economic: even an industrialized aged-account market has to produce accounts whose MULTIPLE independent timelines converge (GitHub server-side history + npm/PyPI releases + Wayback captures + domain age + indexed writing) AND whose code substance actually matches the specific skill claimed for a specific candidate's specific JD. A generic aged account doesn't have a deployed product at a live URL that's been archived since 2021 demonstrating the exact stack — bespoke convergent fakes are expensive per-candidate, which keeps the asymmetry alive for the marginal fraudster even after it's breached for a determined one. Second, behavioral: bought accounts share fingerprints — bursty backfilled commit patterns, no real collaborator graph, no merged PRs into third-party org repos (you can't buy someone else's org accepting your PRs retroactively), absent issue/review interaction. We can flag 'organically-aged vs backfilled' as its own signal. The brutally honest position: against a state-level or well-funded adversary willing to spend real money per candidate, we degrade — but at that spend level they could also just learn the skill, and the cost of faking convergent multi-year provenance approaches the cost of genuine competence, which is the point. We win against the 95% (keyword-stuffers, fork-fakers, backdaters) and we should claim that, not claim invincibility.

### 12. Confidence scores are the easiest thing in the world to fake-calibrate. You emit a 0–1 confidence on each card. Where does that number come from, is it the LLM's self-reported confidence (which is meaningless), and have you validated it against any ground truth — or is it a vibe number that looks rigorous on a slide?
*severity: nice to nail*

Straight answer: in the current PoC, confidence is a structured composite, not the LLM's self-reported probability — and self-reported LLM confidence IS meaningless, so we deliberately don't use it raw. The composite is built from countable evidence properties: number of independent corroborating sources, diff substance/volume relevant to the claim, timeline convergence strength (how many independent clocks agree), and authorship clarity (authored vs forked/ambiguous). High confidence requires multiple independent strong signals; a single source caps confidence regardless of how certain the model 'feels.' The honest admission you're fishing for: we have NOT yet validated this against labeled ground truth, because we don't have a labeled fraud/legit dataset yet — so today's confidence is a principled heuristic, not a calibrated probability, and presenting it as calibrated would be dishonest. The right next step, which we'd do with Redrob's data, is exactly that calibration: Redrob HAS ground truth (the ~80 honeypots, plus hiring outcomes), so we can measure precision/recall of verdicts and reliability-diagram the confidence, then recalibrate. So: the number is defensible as a transparent composite of countable evidence, not a vibe — but I'd lose credibility if I called it validated, and I'm not.

### 13. Failure modes of your dependencies. You're built on GitHub API, Jina Reader, Exa, and Playwright — all external services with rate limits, outages, ToS, and cost. What happens to a candidate's verdict when Jina is down, GitHub rate-limits you mid-batch, or a candidate's site is temporarily 503? Do you fail-open (everyone verified) or fail-closed (everyone unverified)? Both are bad.
*severity: nice to nail*

Neither fail-open nor fail-closed — we fail to an explicit 'indeterminate/retry' state, which is the only honest option and is distinct from a real verdict. Design: (1) A verdict is only emitted when the evidence fetch for that claim actually succeeded; if a dependency is down, the card for that skill is marked 'pending — evidence unavailable,' queued for retry, and never silently coerced into verified or unverified, because both would corrupt the trust signal. (2) Transient failures (503, rate-limit, timeout) are distinguished from genuine absence (404, account-doesn't-exist) — a 503 on a candidate's site is 'retry,' a 404 is evidence of non-liveness. Conflating them is a bug we explicitly guard against. (3) GitHub rate-limit is managed by authenticated requests, batching, and the async-on-shortlist design so we're never hammering at full-funnel volume; mid-batch limit hits pause and resume, they don't fabricate verdicts. (4) Dependency outage degrades coverage (some cards pending), never correctness (no false verdicts). The cost/ToS flag we'd raise to Redrob upfront: these are paid external dependencies with quotas, so at scale we'd want our own GitHub app token and budget caps, and a fallback for Jina/Exa. The principle: the system is allowed to say 'I couldn't check this yet'; it is never allowed to guess and call it a verdict.
